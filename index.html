Slope aspect and forest type as controls on early-winter microclimate and topsoil

Four forest plots on one contour line of the hill in the Forest Botanical Garden, Göttingen: north- and south-facing, coniferous and deciduous. Elevation is held constant, so differences between the plots come from aspect, forest type, or both together.

Main question

How do slope aspect and forest type, alone and together, shape early-winter microclimate and topsoil at constant elevation?

Hypotheses and research questions

HypothesisResearch questionReason
H1 South-facing plots have more light at the forest floor, a higher daily maximum and larger daily range of air, surface and topsoil temperature, and lower relative humidity and soil moisture than north-facing plots, especially on clear days; the difference between coniferous and deciduous plots is larger on the south-facing slope; and the O-horizon is thicker on the north-facing slope, thickest in N-C and thinnest in S-D. How do slope aspect and its interaction with forest type govern forest-floor microclimate and O-horizon accumulation? At Göttingen’s latitude, the low December sun (~16° noon elevation) leaves ~20° north-facing slopes almost entirely shaded, whereas south-facing slopes intercept direct solar radiation that warms and dries the surface—a contrast most pronounced on clear days given sparse winter sunshine. On south-facing slopes, this low-angle light penetrates leafless deciduous canopies but is intercepted by dense evergreen conifers, creating a sharp microclimatic divergence; on north-facing slopes, terrain shading restricts both stand types to diffuse light, minimizing canopy differences. Because litter decomposition accelerates with warmth in this humid climate, cooler north-facing conditions retard decay and promote organic buildup, compounding with recalcitrant needle litter to yield the thickest O-horizon in N-C and the thinnest in S-D (a spatial correspondence two weeks of logger data can evaluate, though not prove causally).
H2 Compared with deciduous forests, coniferous forests exhibit a thicker O-horizon, lower mineral soil pH (particularly in the upper mineral soil), less light at the forest floor, a smaller daily temperature range, and drier topsoil in early winter. How does forest type affect O-horizon thickness, mineral soil pH, and early-winter microclimate (light, daily temperature range, and topsoil moisture)? Needle litter is acidic and poor in nutrients, so it decomposes slowly and builds up into a thicker forest floor than under deciduous trees. Deciduous litter often decomposes faster because of its tissue chemistry, moister soil, and good conditions for earthworms. Decomposing needle litter releases organic acids into the mineral soil, and conifer stands receive more nitrogen and sulphur through throughfall than deciduous stands, acidifying the soil further—though carbonate buffering on calcareous garden soils may limit the pH effect to the top few centimetres. In November and December, deciduous trees are leafless while evergreen conifers still block light. Because forests insulate the understorey and a denser canopy buffers temperatures more, while mean soil temperatures often differ little or only slightly between forest types, we compare daily temperature ranges rather than means. Evergreen conifers also let less precipitation reach the soil, leading to drier topsoil.

Null hypotheses: no difference between aspects and no aspect × forest-type interaction (H1); no difference between forest types (H2). Each combination is one stand, so results are comparisons of four stands; see Methods.

The study site

The Forest Botanical Garden lies on the south-western slope of the Faßberg in the north of Göttingen. Its soils are not natural: when the garden was laid out in the early 1970s, soil was moved to shape the terrain, and clay-rich, calcareous, stony topsoil from a building site in north Göttingen was brought in over the Middle and Lower Muschelkalk (University of Göttingen).

Because the topsoil was partly imported, the soil profile at each plot has to show that the four plots stand on comparable material before differences can be put down to aspect or forest type. The garden’s bioclimatology station records weather continuously and can serve as the open-field reference.
