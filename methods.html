What we measure, where, and why

Four plots, one per combination of aspect and forest type, all on the same contour line. At each plot the same sensor stack records microclimate for two weeks, and one soil profile with auger cores describes the topsoil.

  • Plots: about 20 × 20 m each (N-C, N-D, S-C, S-D), on one contour line taken from the 1 m DGM and checked with GPS.
  • Comparable plots: similar slope angle, at least one tree height from stand edges and paths, loggers at least 5 m apart.
  • Same soil material: the profile and auger cores have to show comparable parent material at all four plots, because part of the garden’s topsoil was brought in.

The role of pH

Soil pH is the main chemical signal of forest type in our design, and it links the litter to the biology of the soil. Which processes buffer the acids depends on the pH range the soil is in (Ulrich 1981):

Buffer rangepHWhat happens
Carbonate8.6–6.2Lime (CaCO₃) neutralises the acid; the widest buffer. Rich soil life, mull humus.
Silicate6.2–5.0Weathering of feldspars, micas and other silicates neutralises the acid slowly.
Exchanger5.0–4.2Clay minerals release their exchangeable base cations; Ca, Mg and K become scarce.
Aluminium, ironbelow 4.2Aluminium dissolves and can reach toxic levels; it binds phosphorus. Below about 3.8 iron dissolves too. Moder and raw humus.

Why it matters for us

  • Forest type (H2): needle litter and the extra nitrogen and sulphur deposition under conifers acidify the topsoil.
  • Soil biology (H1, H2): earthworms avoid acidic soils; less mixing means a thicker O-horizon and a moder or mor humus form.
  • Nutrients: a lower pH means fewer exchangeable base cations and, below 4.2, dissolved aluminium.

What to expect on this site

The garden lies on Muschelkalk with calcareous, clay-rich fill, and limestone is reached at 40–90 cm. Where carbonates remain, the mineral soil stays in the carbonate range and pH hardly changes, even under conifers. Acidification under conifers is then expected mainly in the O-horizon and the top few centimetres. That is what the two pH depths can show.

Measure all samples the same way: CaCl₂ values are lower than values in water, so never mix the two.

Soil texture with the auger cores

Yes: texture is determined by hand on the auger cores and in the spade pit, horizon by horizon. The cores also give the pH samples, so texture and pH come from the same spots.

  1. At each of the 4 sampling points, measure the O-horizon (L, Of, Oh) before augering.
  2. Take an auger core to the limestone or to the auger’s length; lay it out and mark the horizon boundaries.
  3. For each horizon: depth, stones, roots, worm channels.
  4. Finger test for each horizon: sand feels gritty, silt floury and soapy, clay sticky and plastic with a shine when rubbed; assign the texture class with the German soil mapping guide (Bodenkundliche Kartieranleitung) used in the course.
  5. Take the pH samples from the cores at the two mineral-soil depths.
  6. At the spade pit: describe the profile and the humus form in detail.

Why texture matters here

  • Comparability: if one plot sits on clayey fill and another on loess, moisture and pH differences could come from the soil, not from aspect or forest type.
  • Water: clay and silt hold more water, so texture shifts soil moisture independently of the canopy.
  • Buffering: clay and carbonates set how far pH can fall.
  • Calibration: TOMST moisture readings need a soil-specific calibration, chosen by texture class.

  2. Thu 3 Dec09:00–16:00 · vegetation and soils

    Spade pit and 4 auger cores per plot: horizons, texture, O-horizon, humus form; 32 pH samples; check the loggers.

  3. Thu 10 Dec09:00–12:00 · deinstallation

    Retrieve all loggers and read them out with TOMST Lolly and HOBOware. No soil work is left for this half day.

Analysis plan

  1. For each logger and each of the 13 full days: daily mean, maximum, minimum and range.
  2. Average the loggers of each plot; keep their spread.
  3. Effects: aspect (south − north), forest type (coniferous − deciduous) and interaction ((S-D − N-D) − (S-C − N-C)).
  4. Split days into clear and overcast with the weather reference; test H1 on clear days.
  5. Paired daily differences between plots over the 13 days (for example S-D minus N-D), with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test.
  6. Soil: means and spread of the 4 replicates of pH and O-horizon per plot.
What the design can and cannot show. Each combination of aspect and forest type is one stand, so the loggers within a plot are subsamples, not independent replicates (Hurlbert 1984). We therefore report the results as a comparison of four stands, with effect sizes and the spread between loggers, and do not claim a general aspect or forest-type effect.
